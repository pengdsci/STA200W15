Question 1
Which statement best describes a completely randomized design (CRD)?
  1. Experimental units are grouped into homogeneous blocks before random assignment.
  2. Experimental units are randomly assigned to treatment groups.
  3. Each experimental unit receives every treatment in a random order.
  4. Treatments are assigned systematically to avoid randomness.

Correct answer: B

Justification: The note states: “In CRD, experimental units are randomly assigned to different treatment groups.” Random assignment is the defining feature of a CRD.

Why the others are incorrect:

  • A: Blocking is a feature of randomized block designs, not a CRD.
  • C: Receiving every treatment describes a repeated-measures or crossover design.
  • D: Systematic assignment is the opposite of randomization and does not define a CRD.
Question 2
What are the correct null and alternative hypotheses for a one-way ANOVA with t treatments?
  1. H0: σ12 = σ22 = ⋯ = σt2 vs. Ha: at least one variance differs.
  2. H0: μ1 = μ2 = ⋯ = μt vs. Ha: all means are different.
  3. H0: μ1 = μ2 = ⋯ = μt vs. Ha: at least one mean differs.
  4. H0: μ1 ≠ μ2 ≠ ⋯ ≠ μt vs. Ha: all means are equal.

Correct answer: C

Justification: The note gives the CRD hypothesis as H0: μ1 = μ2 = ⋯ = μt vs. Ha: at least one mean differs.

Why the others are incorrect:

  • A: ANOVA tests means, not variances, although it uses variance estimates.
  • B: The alternative does not require all means to be different; only at least one differs.
  • D: This reverses the null and alternative and incorrectly uses “not equal” chains.
Question 3
Which sum-of-squares decomposition is correct in one-way ANOVA?
  1. SST = SSB × SSW
  2. SST = SSB + SSW
  3. SSB = SST + SSW
  4. SSW = SST + SSB

Correct answer: B

Justification: The note derives SST = SSW + SSB, where SST is the total sum of squared residuals, SSW is within-treatment error, and SSB is between-treatment error.

Why the others are incorrect:

  • A: Sums of squares are added, not multiplied.
  • C: SSB is a component of SST, not the sum of SST and SSW.
  • D: SSW is also a component of SST, not the sum of SST and SSB.
Question 4
Suppose a CRD has t = 4 treatments and a total of nT = 40 observations. What are the numerator and denominator degrees of freedom for the ANOVA F-test?
  1. dfN = 4, dfD = 40
  2. dfN = 3, dfD = 36
  3. dfN = 3, dfD = 40
  4. dfN = 4, dfD = 36

Correct answer: B

Justification: The between-treatment degrees of freedom are t − 1 = 4 − 1 = 3. The within-treatment degrees of freedom are nT − t = 40 − 4 = 36. These are the numerator and denominator degrees of freedom of the F-statistic.

Why the others are incorrect:

  • A: Uses t instead of t − 1 and nT instead of nT − t.
  • C: Correct numerator df, but denominator should be nT − t, not nT.
  • D: Reverses the correct values for the two df terms.
Question 5
What does MSW estimate in a one-way ANOVA?
  1. The variance of the treatment sample means.
  2. A weighted average of the within-treatment variances — the pooled within-group variance.
  3. The total variance of all observations ignoring treatments.
  4. The difference between the largest and smallest treatment means.

Correct answer: B

Justification: The note says: “MSW is the weighted average of the variances of each individual treatment.” It is the pooled within-treatment variance and estimates the common error variance when ANOVA assumptions hold.

Why the others are incorrect:

  • A: MSB, not MSW, is related to the variance of the sample means.
  • C: Total variance is represented by SST, not MSW.
  • D: A range is not an ANOVA mean square.
Question 6
If the ANOVA null hypothesis is true, what is the expected relationship between MSB and MSW?
  1. MSB should be much larger than MSW.
  2. MSB and MSW should both estimate the same underlying variance, so their ratio should be approximately 1.
  3. MSW should be exactly 0.
  4. MSB / MSW should be exactly 0.

Correct answer: B

Justification: The note explains: “If there is no real difference between treatments (i.e., H0 is true), MSB and MSW both estimate the same underlying variance. Therefore MSB ≈ MSW.” The F-ratio is therefore near 1 under H0.

Why the others are incorrect:

  • A: Large MSB relative to MSW suggests a treatment effect, not H0.
  • C: MSW is a variance estimate and is not expected to be 0.
  • D: An F-ratio near 0 is not the usual null expectation; near 1 is.
Question 7
The ANOVA F-statistic is often written as F = MSB / MSW = [SSB / (t − 1)] / [SSW / (nT − t)]. What are the numerator and denominator degrees of freedom?
  1. dfN = nT − t, dfD = t − 1
  2. dfN = t − 1, dfD = nT − t
  3. dfN = t, dfD = nT
  4. dfN = nT, dfD = t

Correct answer: B

Justification: The note defines F = [SSB / (t − 1)] / [SSW / (nT − t)] ∼ Ft−1, nT−t, so the numerator df is t − 1 and the denominator df is nT − t.

Why the others are incorrect:

  • A: Reverses the numerator and denominator df.
  • C: Uses t and nT rather than the correct df expressions.
  • D: Also uses the wrong df values and reverses them.
Question 8
Which statement about the F-distribution used in ANOVA is correct?
  1. It is symmetric and can take negative values.
  2. It is right-skewed and defined on nonnegative values.
  3. F7,5 and F5,7 are the same distribution.
  4. The order of the degrees of freedom does not matter.

Correct answer: B

Justification: The note states that FdfN, dfD is “skewed to the right” and that F7,5 and F5,7 are two different F-distributions.

Why the others are incorrect:

  • A: The F-distribution is right-skewed, not symmetric, and cannot be negative.
  • C: The note explicitly warns that F7,5 ≠ F5,7.
  • D: The order of numerator and denominator df must be respected.
Question 9
In R, which command gives the upper-tail p-value for an ANOVA F-statistic of 3.5 with dfN = 3 and dfD = 20?
  1. pf(3.5, 20, 3, lower.tail = FALSE)
  2. pf(3.5, 3, 20, lower.tail = FALSE)
  3. qf(3.5, 3, 20)
  4. pf(3.5, 3, 20, lower.tail = TRUE)

Correct answer: B

Justification: ANOVA uses the upper tail of the F-distribution. The R syntax is pf(F.value, dfN, dfD, lower.tail = FALSE). Thus:

pf(3.5, 3, 20, lower.tail = FALSE)
## [1] 0.0340

This gives an upper-tail p-value of approximately 0.034.

Why the others are incorrect:

  • A: Reverses the df arguments.
  • C: qf() gives a critical value, not a p-value.
  • D: lower.tail = TRUE gives the lower-tail probability, not the ANOVA upper-tail p-value.
Question 10
The following R output is from anova(aov.model):
Analysis of Variance Table
Response: Length

           Df Sum Sq Mean Sq F value Pr(>F)
Treatment   4 1105.7  276.43  52.642 < 2.2e-16 ***
Residuals  45  236.3    5.251

At α = 0.05, what is the correct conclusion?

  1. Fail to reject H0; all treatment means are equal.
  2. Reject H0; at least one treatment mean differs.
  3. Conclude that all treatment variances are equal.
  4. The ANOVA table cannot be used to test treatment effects.

Correct answer: B

Justification: The p-value is < 2.2e-16, which is far below 0.05. The note’s decision rule says to reject H0 when the p-value is less than α. Therefore, at least one treatment mean differs.

Why the others are incorrect:

  • A: The p-value is very small, so failing to reject is wrong.
  • C: This ANOVA tests means, not equality of variances.
  • D: The ANOVA table is precisely the standard tool for this test.
Question 11
Why does the R function aov() require the data in long format?
  1. Because aov() cannot handle balanced data.
  2. Because aov() requires one column for the response and a separate column identifying the treatment group for each observation.
  3. Because aov() only accepts matrix input.
  4. Because wide-format data cannot be converted to long format.

Correct answer: B

Justification: The note explains: “aov() requires a long table structure. That is, all measurements of the response must be stored in a column and a standalone column to label the treatment of each value.”

Why the others are incorrect:

  • A: aov() handles balanced data perfectly well; the issue is data layout.
  • C: aov() uses a model formula and data frame, not matrix-only input.
  • D: Wide data can be reshaped to long format.
Question 12
Tukey’s HSD output includes:
                  diff        lwr        upr     p adj
Glucose-Control  -10.9 -13.811928  -7.988072 0.0000000

What is the correct interpretation?

  1. Glucose and Control are not significantly different.
  2. Glucose is significantly lower than Control by about 10.9 units.
  3. Glucose is significantly higher than Control by about 10.9 units.
  4. The confidence interval contains 0, so no difference exists.

Correct answer: B

Justification: In Tukey output, diff = Glucose − Control = −10.9. The negative sign means Glucose’s mean is lower than Control’s mean. The adjusted p-value is essentially 0, and the 95% interval [−13.81, −7.99] excludes 0, so the difference is significant.

Why the others are incorrect:

  • A: The p-value is near 0, indicating significance.
  • C: The negative sign indicates Glucose is lower, not higher.
  • D: The interval does not contain 0.
Question 13
Another Tukey row is:
                  diff        lwr      upr     p adj
Fructose-Glucose  -1.3 -4.211928 1.611928 0.7114379

What should you conclude?

  1. Fructose and Glucose differ significantly because the mean difference is negative.
  2. Fructose and Glucose do not differ significantly because the adjusted p-value is large and the interval includes 0.
  3. The interval proves the two means are exactly equal.
  4. Tukey’s HSD cannot be used for this pair.

Correct answer: B

Justification: The adjusted p-value is 0.711, well above 0.05, and the 95% confidence interval [−4.21, 1.61] includes 0. The note says there is “no significant difference” for pairs such as Fructose–Glucose.

Why the others are incorrect:

  • A: A negative point estimate alone does not establish significance.
  • C: A confidence interval including 0 does not prove exact equality; it only fails to show a significant difference.
  • D: Tukey’s HSD is designed for all pairwise comparisons.
Question 14
What is the main purpose of the Bonferroni correction in post-hoc comparisons?
  1. To increase the Type I error rate.
  2. To control the family-wise error rate by adjusting p-values upward for the number of comparisons.
  3. To replace the ANOVA F-test.
  4. To test homogeneity of variance.

Correct answer: B

Justification: The note says Bonferroni “adjusts p-values by multiplying them by the number of comparisons” and is used to control the family-wise error rate. It is a conservative multiple-comparison procedure.

Why the others are incorrect:

  • A: Bonferroni reduces the risk of Type I error inflation; it does not increase it.
  • C: It is a post-hoc procedure performed after ANOVA, not a replacement for the F-test.
  • D: Homogeneity of variance is checked with tests such as Levene’s or Bartlett’s test, not Bonferroni.
Question 15
When should post-hoc multiple-comparison tests such as Tukey’s HSD or Bonferroni be used?
  1. Always, regardless of the ANOVA result.
  2. Only after the omnibus ANOVA F-test is significant.
  3. Only when the ANOVA F-test is not significant.
  4. Before fitting the ANOVA model.

Correct answer: B

Justification: The note explains that a significant F-test indicates that at least one group mean differs, but it does not identify which pairs differ. Post-hoc tests are then used “to identify these specific differences.” If the omnibus test is not significant, there is no strong evidence of any difference to investigate.

Why the others are incorrect:

  • A: Running all pairwise comparisons without a significant omnibus result can inflate Type I error and is not the recommended sequence.
  • C: Post-hoc tests are used after a significant result, not a nonsignificant one.
  • D: Post-hoc comparisons require a fitted ANOVA model first.
Question 16
Which of the following is not a fundamental assumption of one-way ANOVA?
  1. Observations are independent.
  2. Data in each group are approximately normally distributed.
  3. The populations have equal variances.
  4. All treatment group means must be equal.

Correct answer: D

Justification: The note lists the assumptions as independence, approximate normality within groups, and homogeneity of variance. “All group means are equal” is the null hypothesis, not an assumption required for the test to be valid.

Why the others are incorrect:

  • A: Independence is explicitly an ANOVA assumption.
  • B: Normality within groups is explicitly an ANOVA assumption.
  • C: Homogeneity of variance is explicitly an ANOVA assumption.
Question 17
If the treatment effect is large, what pattern should you expect in the ANOVA mean squares?
  1. MSB should tend to be much larger than MSW, producing a large F-statistic.
  2. MSW should tend to be much larger than MSB, producing a large F-statistic.
  3. MSB and MSW should be exactly equal.
  4. The F-statistic should be close to 0.

Correct answer: A

Justification: MSB reflects variability between treatment means. When treatment means differ substantially, MSB becomes large relative to MSW, and F = MSB / MSW becomes large, providing evidence against H0.

Why the others are incorrect:

  • B: If MSW were much larger, the F-ratio would be small, not large.
  • C: Exact equality is not expected even under H0 because of sampling variability.
  • D: A large treatment effect pushes F upward, not toward 0.
Question 18
Which R command performs Bonferroni-adjusted pairwise comparisons for the pea-length data?
  1. TukeyHSD(aov.model)
  2. pairwise.t.test(x = pea.long$Length, g = pea.long$Treatment, p.adjust.method = "bonferroni")
  3. aov(Length ~ Treatment, data = pea.long)
  4. pf(F.value, dfN, dfD, lower.tail = FALSE)

Correct answer: B

Justification: The note uses:

pairwise.t.test(
  x = pea.long$Length,
  g = pea.long$Treatment,
  p.adjust.method = "bonferroni"
)

to obtain Bonferroni-adjusted pairwise p-values.

Why the others are incorrect:

  • A: TukeyHSD() performs Tukey’s HSD, not Bonferroni.
  • C: aov() fits the ANOVA model but does not perform pairwise comparisons.
  • D: pf() computes an F-distribution tail probability and is not a pairwise comparison procedure.
Question 19
In the pea-length example, SSB is computed as:
SS.B = 10 * sum(c(
  (mean(Control)   - ybar..)^2,
  (mean(Glucose)   - ybar..)^2,
  (mean(Fructose)  - ybar..)^2,

  (mean(Sucrose)   - ybar..)^2

Why is the multiplier 10 used?

  1. Because there are 10 treatments.
  2. Because each treatment has 10 replicates in this balanced CRD.
  3. Because the total sample size is 10.
  4. Because the grand variance is 10.

Correct answer: B

Justification: In a balanced CRD with ni = 10 observations per treatment, each squared deviation of a treatment mean from the grand mean must be weighted by the treatment sample size:

SSB = ∑i=1t ni (ȳi· − ȳ··)2.

Here every ni = 10.

Why the others are incorrect:

  • A: There are t = 5 treatments, not 10.
  • C: The total sample size is nT = 50, not 10.
  • D: The multiplier is the number of replicates, not a variance value.
Question 20
The pea-length ANOVA table reports:

269.486 / 5.251
## [1] 51.31981
